\title{Geometry-Encoded Hidden Orientational Memory}

\author{Vladimir A. Baulin}
\affiliation{Synthetix Institute, Amsterdam, The Netherlands}
\email{vbaulin@synthetix.institute}

\date{}

\begin{abstract}
Materials can retain traces of past forcing through structural rearrangements,
hysteretic switching, persistent templates, or slow collective modes. Here we
ask whether a material can remember a spatial arrangement of particle
orientations when the particle centres do not rearrange, the axes continue to
rotate by Brownian motion, and the field that prepared the arrangement has
been removed. We study elongated colloids whose centres are caged at a liquid
interface. Quadrupolar capillary deformations couple the orientations of each
pair to the line joining their centres. In a disordered layer, different bonds
favour incompatible orientations. This competition prevents a common director
and produces many angular compromise states. Simulations show that two copies
prepared in the same state retain an angular overlap of $0.462\pm0.010$ after
625 rotational-diffusion times. A pattern prepared by a temporary field retains
an overlap of $0.441\pm0.021$ after that field is removed. A matched system
without the capillary interaction forgets both arrangements within a few
rotational times. Spatial correlations end within two particle spacings, and
finite-size tests find no equilibrium spin-glass crossing over the sizes
examined. Retention is largest near the disorder at which global nematic order
collapses. Fixed bond geometry can therefore preserve a particle-by-particle
orientation pattern even though the particles continue to rotate and no local
alignment field remains.
\end{abstract}

\maketitle

Materials often retain traces of how they were driven \cite{keim2019}. A cyclically sheared
suspension rearranges until its particle trajectories reflect the training
amplitude \cite{keim2011,paulsen2014}. A hysteretic element remains on one of
several metastable branches after the load is removed. Defects or quenched
local fields can preserve a preferred state, while glassy modes can carry a
slowly fading history \cite{rotunno2005}. These mechanisms store the
trace in different physical objects: a new particle arrangement, a selected
energy minimum, a permanent local preference, or a slow collective mode.

Caged colloidal ellipsoids pose a different problem. Their centres can remain
fixed while their long axes continue to rotate by Brownian motion
\cite{zheng2011,roller2021}. The particle arrangement then provides a stable
network of neighbours without itself recording the orientation of every
particle. Can interactions on that network preserve a particular angular
arrangement prepared by a temporary field? We test this possibility in a
disordered layer of interfacial ellipsoids. After the field is removed, the
prepared arrangement must remain recognizable and must persist longer than in
a matched system without the proposed capillary interaction. This comparison
separates memory of the preparation from rotational motion that is merely slow.

Bond-frame coupling supplies the required mechanism. Nematic alignment favors
\(\theta_i-\theta_j=0\). A capillary bond instead favors a value of
\(\theta_i+\theta_j-2\phi_{ij}\), where \(\phi_{ij}\) is the bond direction.
Several differently oriented bonds cannot generally satisfy both preferences
with one director. Each bond can remain locally ordered, but the conditions
accumulated around intersecting loops need not close on one orientation field.
The rotors then settle into graph-dependent compromise states. A temporary
field selects one compromise, and interaction barriers preserve that choice
after release. The resulting memory is hidden from a bulk polarizer but
remains visible in a particle-resolved overlap.

Interfacial ellipsoids provide this interaction directly. Their leading
contact-line deformation is quadrupolar,
\(u_i\propto\Delta u_{\max}(R/r)^2\cos2(\phi-\theta_i)\). Meniscus overlap
gives the pair potential
\(U^{\rm cap}_{ij}=-A_4(r_{ij})\cos2(\theta_i+\theta_j-2\phi_{ij})\)
\cite{loudet2005,luo2019}. Its amplitude is
\(A_4=3\pi\gamma(\Delta u_{\max})^2(R/r_{ij})^4\). Here \(\gamma\) is the interfacial tension and
\(R\) is a particle length scale. This static deformation supplies a
persistent pair torque without a one-particle easy axis.
For \(A_4>0\), an isolated pair minimizes its energy when
\(\theta_i+\theta_j=2\phi_{ij}+m\pi\). A particle in a dense layer belongs to
several such pairs. Disorder changes their bond angles, so one orientation
cannot minimize every adjacent interaction. This realizes geometric
frustration using the particle positions themselves.

The contact-line meniscus in panel (a) supplies a pair constraint rather than
a one-particle easy axis (Fig.~\ref{fig:realization}). The neighbour graph in panel (b) remains fixed while
the rod angles undergo Brownian rotation. Nanometre contact-line amplitudes
place the far-field torque in the thermal range, $1$--$5k_{\rm B}T$
[Fig.~\ref{fig:realization}(c)], without patterned grooves. Below this window,
thermal rotation erases bond registration; far above it, capillary attraction
immobilizes or aggregates the particles.

The competing bond preferences enter the energy of \(N\) apolar Brownian
rotors on a fixed neighbour graph,
\begin{align}
 U={}&-\sum_{(ij)}J w^-_{ij}\cos2(\theta_i-\theta_j)\nonumber\\
 &-\sum_{(ij)}g w^+_{ij}
 \cos2(\theta_i+\theta_j-2\phi_{ij}).
 \label{eq:model}
\end{align}
The first term is the leading short-range apolar alignment, with energy
\(J\) and weight \(w^-_{ij}\). It can arise from steric, near-field capillary,
or other shape-dependent interactions. The second term is the quadrupolar
channel, with \(g=A_4(r_0)\) and
\(w^+_{ij}=\min[6,(r_0/r_{ij})^4]\). The median nearest-neighbour distance is
\(r_0\). Particle centres are displaced from a triangular cage by a Gaussian
width \(\sigma\); no orientational blocks are imposed. The Supplemental
Material specifies both weights, graph construction, and simulation schedule.
The graph remains fixed during each angular trajectory, while the orientations
remain Brownian. Equation~\eqref{eq:model} therefore isolates angular memory
from positional rearrangement. Temporary writing fields are absent during
every reported retention interval.

The angular density obeys the rotational Smoluchowski equation. Its It\^o
Langevin form is integrated with rotational diffusivity \(D_r\). Global,
relative-pair, and bond-frame order are
\begin{align}
 S&=\left|N^{-1}\sum_i e^{2i\theta_i}\right|,\nonumber\\
 C_2&=\frac{\sum_{(ij)}w^-_{ij}\cos2(\theta_i-\theta_j)}
 {\sum_{(ij)}w^-_{ij}},\nonumber\\
 G_2&=\frac{\sum_{(ij)}w^+_{ij}
 \cos2(\theta_i+\theta_j-2\phi_{ij})}{\sum_{(ij)}w^+_{ij}}.
 \label{eq:order}
\end{align}
Equation~\eqref{eq:order} separates common-director order from relative-angle
and bond-frame organization. The finite-window statistic
\(q_{\rm EA}=N^{-1}\sum_i|\langle e^{2i\theta_i}\rangle_t|^2\) measures
persistence, not memory by itself. Memory is the post-release overlap with a
known preparation. We use
\(Q_{\rm split}=N^{-1}\sum_i\cos2(\theta_i^{a}-\theta_i^{b})\) for descendants
with independent noise. We use
\(Q_{\rm target}=N^{-1}\sum_i\cos2(\theta_i-\theta_i^{\rm target})\) for a
written state.
The first protocol asks whether two descendants inherit the same basin. The
second asks whether a specified preparation remains readable. We call the
response hidden orientational memory only when \(S\) is small, local
correlations are finite, and a post-release overlap survives. Equilibrium
replica tests are evaluated separately.

Competing bond preferences produce a mixed regime with local order but no
common director. Figure~\ref{fig:map} resolves four finite-size response
classes in the observable vector
\((S,C_2,G_2,q_{\rm EA},C_{\rm window})\). The coupling
coordinates are withheld from clustering. All 27 graph bootstraps select four
classes. Dropping either persistence coordinate still selects four, with
adjusted Rand indices 0.821 and 0.805. The map is therefore a dynamical
taxonomy, not a thermodynamic phase diagram.
The four classes distinguish a Brownian crossover, relative alignment,
capillary-frame persistence, and a mixed state. Both pair harmonics remain
finite in the mixed state while \(S\) is small. This population contains 169
of 441 cells. Its centroid is
\((S,C_2,G_2,q_{\rm EA})=(0.112,0.638,0.414,0.628)\).
The marked point lies inside this population and was selected before the
larger graphs and long trajectories were analyzed.

The arrangement of the four classes follows directly from the competing
terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}. Weak \(J\) and \(g\) give the Brownian
crossover. Large \(J\) with weak \(g\) selects relative alignment and a common
director. Large \(g\) with weak \(J\) registers orientations to the bond
frame but weakens neighbour-parallel order. The mixed class occupies the
region where neither interaction eliminates the other. In Fig.~\ref{fig:map}(b),
the dark bands are cells nearly equidistant from two centroids. They measure
classification ambiguity, not thermodynamic coexistence. The cut in panel (c)
shows how increasing \(J\) builds \(C_2\) while \(G_2\) remains finite. The
marked state lies beyond this crossover, where both constraints act but
global order remains weak.

The low-order mixed regime persists on the larger graphs used for the memory
tests. At the marked point, five \(N=1024\) graphs give
\((S,C_2,G_2,q_{\rm EA})=(0.067,0.627,0.447,0.658)\). Graph standard
deviations are below 0.011. Removing the capillary term sets \(G_2\) to zero and
reduces \(q_{\rm EA}\) to \(0.022\), even though \(S\) and \(C_2\) increase.
On a regular graph the same couplings instead give \(S=0.945\). Thus the
bond-frame term suppresses a common director while preserving local angular
constraints. Positional disorder resolves a finite memory window: retention
peaks near \(\sigma=0.11a\), where nematic order first collapses
(Supplemental Figs.~S3 and S4).
Across the wider disorder scan, \(S\) falls thirteenfold, from 0.916 to 0.069,
whereas \(q_{\rm EA}\) changes by only a factor of 1.3. Above the retention
maximum, the connected written overlap decreases from \(0.559\pm0.022\) at
\(\sigma=0.11a\) to \(0.386\pm0.026\) at \(0.28a\). Disorder first removes
the common director. Beyond the maximum, it also weakens the compatible local
network that carries the written pattern.

Post-release dynamics establish memory beyond static bond registration.
Figure~\ref{fig:dynamics}(a) gives
\(Q_{\rm split}=0.462\pm0.010\) at \(D_rt=625\); the matched control falls
below 0.2 by \(D_rt=2.7\pm0.3\). A temporary field produces
\(Q_{\rm target}=0.863\pm0.009\), followed by
\(0.441\pm0.021\) after release [Fig.~\ref{fig:dynamics}(b)]. The control
erases the same target within \(4.3\pm0.3\) rotational times. The aging
increment is positive on every tested graph. These observations identify a
history-conditioned basin rather than a slowly relaxing interface. The
estimated interface relaxation time is at least three orders of magnitude
shorter (Supplemental Material).

The selected basin becomes more persistent as the preparation ages. At fixed lag
\(D_r\Delta t=30\), the paired increase is
\(0.0253\pm0.0019\), and it is positive on every graph. This finite-size aging
quantifies slow exploration of the same multibasin landscape. Split-replica
and written-state retention therefore establish two complementary facts:
basin identity and a specified preparation both survive field removal.

The retained preparation appears only after the interactions become strong
enough to oppose Brownian escape. Figure~\ref{fig:activated} resolves this
coupling-controlled onset. At the common read time
\(D_rT_{\rm obs}=625\), split-replica overlap is statistically consistent
with zero at \(\lambda=0.30\) and 0.45. It reaches
\(0.405\pm0.008\) at 0.90 and \(0.691\pm0.005\) at 1.40.
Written-state retention reaches \(0.466\pm0.016\) and
\(0.497\pm0.010\) at the same two couplings, whereas both \(g=0\)
controls remain consistent with zero. The capillary bond-frame
interaction is required for retention on this observation scale.

Scaling both interaction terms with \(\lambda\) locates the finite-time
crossover while leaving barrier growth and loop incompatibility coupled.
Panel (c) separates finite-window persistence from memory.
At \(\lambda=0.30\), \(q_{\rm EA}\) falls to \(0.0066\) as the window
approaches \(D_rT=625\). At 1.40 it remains \(0.776\).
The broad onset is a finite-time dynamical crossover rather than a singular
transition.

Finite-size scaling separates this memory from equilibrium glass order.
Between \(N=144\) and 2304, \(S\propto N^{-0.479}\) and independently
equilibrated replica overlap scales as \(N^{-0.466}\). Local order remains
finite, while \(\xi_L/L\) has no common crossing. Spatial correlations reach
the noise floor by \(r/a=1.73\), yet descendants retain overlap for hundreds
of rotational times. The stored variable is therefore local basin identity,
conditioned on a common preparation.
The overlap susceptibility saturates with size, and the largest measured
second-moment correlation length is \(1.49a\). Independent preparations do
not select one macroscopic state. Descendants of the same preparation remain
related because they begin in the same local basin. This is genealogical
memory rather than equilibrium replica order.

The capillary minimum admits a discrete transport description. Transport
across bond \((ij)\) reflects an apolar angle about the bond frame; transport
around a cycle composes these reflections,
\begin{align}
 \mathcal T_{ij}(\theta)&=2\phi_{ij}-\theta\pmod{\pi},\nonumber\\
 \mathcal T_C&=\mathcal T_{i_0i_1}\circ\cdots\circ
 \mathcal T_{i_{n-1}i_0}.
 \label{eq:transport}
\end{align}
The maps in Eq.~\eqref{eq:transport} form a discrete \(O(2)\) connection, and
\(\mathcal T_C\) is its cycle holonomy. One odd cycle produces a reflection
and therefore possesses fixed orientations. Frustration arises when the cycle
maps of the bond-frame graph have no common fixed orientation. This
common-fixed-point condition states precisely when locally preferred bond
frames fail to define one global angular state.

Closed-path incompatibility enlarges the metastable-state catalogue in the
strong-locking limit. The apolar reduction assigns each local region a binary state and
each loop the gauge-invariant product
\(\mathcal W_\gamma\) of its coupling signs. Flattening loops with
\(\mathcal W_\gamma=-1\), while preserving the graph and coupling
magnitudes, removes \(5.58\) one-flip-stable states on average. The reduced
landscape therefore contains additional choices when closed-path
constraints are incompatible.
The intervention changes loop compatibility without changing the graph or
coupling magnitudes. Across 24 graphs, the excess stable-state count has a
bootstrap 95\% interval of 2.67--8.83. Individual written patterns can improve
or worsen when loop mismatch is removed. Thus incompatible loops create
alternatives; they do not guarantee that every target will be retained.

Landscape capacity and retrieval fidelity vary independently. Incompatible
loops enlarge the alphabet of compromise states. A written pattern \(W\) is
captured in a basin \(B_0\), Brownian relaxation carries it to \(B_t\), and a
readout \(R\) identifies what survives: \(W\to B_0\to B_t\to R\). Stable-state
count bounds the available alphabet; capture probabilities and escape rates
set retrieval fidelity. The post-release overlaps measure this
\emph{relaxation-retained incompatibility}, not loop holonomy alone.

The distinction between an available state and a retained state also governs
operation-order memory. Athermal disordered Ising models show that two
protocols reaching identical final controls can leave distinct microstates
\cite{croce2026}. Thermal release adds a second requirement: the two histories
must remain distinguishable after both controls vanish. With independent
Brownian histories at \(N=256\), two opposed write fields are applied as
\(AB\) or \(BA\). They give \(M_{\rm ord}=0.634\pm0.020\) for overlapping
supports and \(0.0123\pm0.0027\) for separated supports. At
\(h=8k_{\rm B}T\), the single-trajectory readout identifies the order in
100\% of overlapping trials and \(55.0\pm1.3\%\) of separated trials. Their discriminabilities are
\(d'=12.55\pm0.97\) and \(0.31\pm0.05\) across five graphs. A nonzero response
during writing is insufficient. The protocol and controls appear in
Supplemental Fig.~S9 and Table~SVI. Field-free relaxation projects the two
driven histories onto their readout classes. The signed post-release overlap
is therefore the material order parameter for sequence.

Overlapping and opposing fields make the sequence observable. Partitioned
fields address disjoint rotors, so reversing their order does not reverse a
shared local decision. Contested fields instead favor opposite orientations
on the same rotors. The last pulse places those coordinates on opposite sides
of a basin boundary, and coupling propagates that choice beyond the written
set. Sequence memory in this realization therefore requires the dynamical
influence of the two operations to overlap on retained coordinates. Transient
noncommutativity matters only through the component that survives field-free
relaxation.

The mechanism requires the timescale separation
\(\tau_{\rm int}\ll D_r^{-1}\ll\tau_{\rm cage}\). Charged rods already show
caged translation with rotational mobility \cite{besseling2026}, and Brownian
colloidal pivots demonstrate accessible angular motion \cite{melio2026}. A
candidate realization is a dense air--water monolayer of polymer ellipsoids.
Electrostatic repulsion and crowding maintain the cage, while roughness and
wetting tune capillary attraction \cite{trevenen2023,rahman2026}. At
\(\gamma=10\,\mathrm{mN\,m^{-1}}\) and \(r/R=3\), the target
\(A_4=1\)--\(5k_{\rm B}T\) requires contact-line amplitudes of
\(\Delta u_{\max}=1.9\)--\(4.2\,\mathrm{nm}\).
The condition \(\tau_{\rm int}\ll D_r^{-1}\) makes the interface follow the
rotor quasistatically. The condition \(D_r^{-1}\ll\tau_{\rm cage}\) allows
the angles to explore their landscape before the
neighbour graph changes. Smooth ellipsoids interact much more strongly, so
the experiment requires an intermediate quadrupole rather than maximal
capillary attraction.

The same interface provides three experimentally distinct controls. A
polarized field writes \(\theta_i\) without moving the centres. Deviatoric
surface curvature supplies a mechanical write torque and vanishes when the
surface is flattened. A spatially uniform capillary wave instead modulates
\(g\), changing the escape barrier without encoding a target. Waves that move
the centres change \(\sigma\) and therefore traverse the retention maximum
near \(0.11a\). These operations predict separate write, gate, and erase
responses that can be distinguished by tracking both positions and angles.

Video microscopy provides every model observable from \(\bm r_i(t)\) and
\(\theta_i(t)\). A two-particle measurement first calibrates \(J\), \(g\),
and the \(r^{-4}\) range. The collective test then perturbs the layer,
removes the perturbation, and compares post-release overlap with a
low-quadrupole control. Polarized traps provide a temporary write torque
\cite{lapointe2011}. Shaped interfaces offer a mechanical alternative because
deviatoric curvature couples directly to a capillary quadrupole
\cite{cavallaro2011}. Correlating local retained overlap with local loop-closure
mismatch would distinguish memory centered on incompatible cores, their
boundaries, or compatible patches.
A pair calibration is essential because \(J\) is not fixed by the far-field
quadrupole. It may contain steric and near-field capillary harmonics. Measuring
\(p(\theta_i,\theta_j\mid r)\) before the collective experiment separates the
two terms in Eq.~\eqref{eq:model}. The same microscope can then verify that
the neighbour graph remains stable throughout the read interval.

Two orthogonal polarization patterns give a direct sequence experiment. The
\(AB\) and \(BA\) histories receive the same duration and optical dose, but in
opposite order. Reading after both fields vanish tests whether the layer acts
as a passive last-event sensor. A transient surface-curvature pattern provides
a nonoptical write channel; flattening the interface removes that torque
before readout.

Finite-time orientational memory persists on the quenched positional graphs
examined here. Translational motion and equilibrium glass order define the
next physical boundaries. Time-step convergence, translational Brownian
motion, and independent-noise sequence decoding provide direct tests of those
boundaries (Supplemental Material).

Particle positions supply a quenched network, capillary quadrupoles define
bond-frame transport, and incompatible loops create alternative angular
compromises. Field-free Smoluchowski dynamics projects a written configuration
into one such compromise, while interaction barriers delay escape. When two
writes contest the same rotors, reversing their order selects different
basins. Geometry therefore stores both a hidden orientational state and the
order of the operations that prepared it.
